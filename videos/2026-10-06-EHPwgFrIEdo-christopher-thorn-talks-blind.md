## Summary

Christopher Thorn, guitarist for Blind Melon, talks with an Irish podcast host about moving from the band into running his own studio, now in Joshua Tree. He says he always planned to make records. In the 90s he toured with a portable rig (Neve, U87, ADATs, Mackie board) and recorded demos in hotel rooms. Some of that work ended up on the third Blind Melon record, *Nico*. He built his first studio in Seattle in 1992 and still uses the 1973 32-channel API console he bought that year. He learned engineering from Andy Wallace and from Mike Npalitano, an assistant at Kingsway Studio, where *Soup* was made. He says *Soup* followed a natural progression from the looser first album. The band's only rule was not to repeat the first album. After the first album sold so well, they barred the label's A&R from the sessions. He calls the result a pure piece of art but says he regrets cutting the label out. He contrasts the MTV-driven monoculture of the 90s with today's fragmented, phone-distracted listening. He also describes his writing habits (late nights, chasing an idea until it's finished, recording everything on his phone) and his glass-free, window-filled, always-plugged-in studio. He lists Zeppelin III, Neil Young, Dylan's *Blood on the Tracks* and the Stones as formative records. The transcript was truncated at about 30,000 of 71,245 characters, so this covers only the first part of the conversation.

For you, the most concrete takeaways are about workflow and studio setup. Keep your instruments, amps and recording gear permanently plugged in so an idea can become a demo quickly. Record every idea on your phone and review the voice memos a week later, because things that seemed unremarkable at the time can turn out to be good. When a song shows up, finish it that night instead of putting it off. Design your space for light and open communication, such as skipping the control-room glass, if you want a more relaxed tracking environment. If you're learning engineering, copy his approach: ask a lot of questions, and test every microphone you have access to so you learn how each one sounds. If you work with a label or other business partners, give them some involvement, because he regrets shutting them out completely. His other suggestions are to listen to full albums, with liner notes, instead of scattered streaming, and to bet on live, in-room music becoming more valuable as AI-generated music spreads.
