## Summary

Snyder argues that history's core purpose is helping you understand your own relationship to the world by recognizing your co-responsibility within it—not as a dominant player, but as someone whose actions constantly relate to how you understand the past. He rejects two false dichotomies about possibility: "anything can happen" (which leads to despair when reality proves limited) and "nothing is possible" (which disempowers from the start). The true historical position is that some futures are possible and some are not; greater knowledge of the past increases your ability to recognize which ones. He also distinguishes actual history—a critical academic discipline based on testing hypotheses against evidence—from "memory politics" or nostalgia, which pose as history while claiming the status quo is inevitable and thereby disempower you. The 1930s–1940s study of Nazism and Stalinism matters precisely because those horrific systems *happened*, proving that mass murder, totalitarianism, and concentration camps are within the human spectrum of possibility, not aberrations.

For you as a student: treat historical knowledge as practical power for understanding the present and shaping the future, not as dead facts. Before engaging with any historical lecture or course, do the assigned readings first—they form the foundation for making sense of the larger arguments. When you encounter political claims about "returning to a better past" or hear that "history repeats," recognize these as disempowering tactics, not history. Instead, study what actually happened to see which possibilities remain open and how your choices can nudge outcomes away from known horrors.
