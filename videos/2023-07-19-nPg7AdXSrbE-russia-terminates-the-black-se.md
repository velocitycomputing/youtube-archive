## Summary

Peter Zeihan discusses the collapse of Ukrainian grain exports and the Black Sea Grain Deal. This is a Russia-Ukraine war analysis stitched from several recordings, so the timeline jumps around. In the first segment (August 2022), the first ship, the Razoni, loaded 26,000 metric tons at Odesa for Lebanon. Ukraine had about 18 million tons in storage and would need roughly 700 such ships. He argues that fuel shortages, full silos, and war-disrupted harvests make Ukrainian participation in global grain markets unlikely to last. Rail can move only about one-fifth of normal volume because of gauge differences, limited rolling stock, and routes through Transnistria. Water shipping is about three times cheaper than rail. Later segments (spring 2023) say Russia now insists on 60-day renewals instead of 120. He expects Russia to shift from attacking the power grid in winter to attacking agricultural infrastructure in summer, and says the deal will not be renewed. Poland, Hungary, Slovakia, Bulgaria and Romania have banned Ukrainian grain imports, though they still allow transit. He estimates that exports will fall 80–90% and that lost sunflower crushing capacity makes this worse. Egypt, parts of Africa and South Asia would be hit hardest. He warns that a doubling of wheat prices triggered the 2011 Arab Spring, but says the US would see only about a 25-cent rise in a loaf of bread. Restoring exports would require Ukraine to retake all of the south and Crimea, which he considers unlikely this year, so Ukraine would not be a major agricultural player for years.

If you hold food, fertilizer or agricultural exposure, or track emerging-market stability (Egypt, Middle East, North Africa), the useful signals he points to are wheat and fertilizer prices (potash, phosphate, nitrogen), Black Sea shipping and insurance conditions, and EU import-ban policy in the five border countries. For personal budgeting, his claim is that US consumers would barely notice, about 25 cents per loaf, so this is not a reason for stockpiling in the US. Treat his forecasts as one analyst's confident projections rather than established facts. Several, such as the "last year" of Ukrainian exports, are stated with more certainty than the evidence supports, and I haven't verified them.
