## Summary

David's question in the video is what scales are actually good for if you only play existing songs. The presenter says a scale is like an alphabet: it gives you the notes but not fluency, so you learn it in context. He gives several uses. A beginner who can play the C major scale can embellish simple songs such as "Let It Be". C major has the same notes as A minor, so it also covers many lead lines. A viewer called Big Dave spent a year learning the "Don't Look Back in Anger" solo note for note. He later found it was a common run of the A minor pentatonic scale, and knowing that first would have made it much easier. The E minor pentatonic scale underlies many songs, including "20th Century Boy", the Cramps' "Fever", "Shakin' All Over" and "Rumble". Because every note in a scale fits together, you can improvise over a backing track without reading tab and without much thought. The presenter plays over the level-two jam track (C major, A minor, F) to show this. He adds that scales fit particular chords, and that this is covered in his lead guitar course and later in the beginner and intermediate syllabuses.

For you, this means that once you can play the C major scale cleanly, you can start using it now. Embellish simple songs with it, and improvise over the level-two jam track (C, A minor, F) in the beginner course, instead of reading tab. Treat the C major and A minor scales as the same set of notes, so the shapes you know already work in both keys. When you reach the E minor pentatonic scale in the course, use it to pick out riffs and lead lines in songs like "20th Century Boy", "Fever" or "Shakin' All Over". The presenter has a video called "10 songs with the E minor pentatonic scale" (he released it in January) if you want examples. When you learn a solo, check whether it is a common scale run, such as A minor pentatonic, before you memorise it note for note. Don't worry about chord-to-scale matching yet. The course introduces it later, and the lead guitar and intermediate electric courses go deeper.
