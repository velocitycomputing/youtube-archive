## Summary

Destin Sandlin of Smarter Every Day tries to recreate the Civil War bullets that met nose-to-nose and fused, which he first saw years ago at the American Museum of Natural History. He and three collaborators (Ernie on electronics, Jeremy Fielding on safety shielding, David on mechanical design and ammunition reloading) build two bolted-down .45 Long Colt barrels fired by electrical primers instead of mechanical hammers. Removing hammer "lock time" lets them control the firing time. A swinging steel blocker lets them load one gun without standing in front of the other. A strict checklist, with misfire procedures that two explosives-testing friends helped refine, governs every shot. They hit bullet-on-bullet on the third shot, but the bullets fragmented instead of fusing. Misses and inconsistent results came from aiming drift, a loose elevation nut on the datum gun, and powder and rifling-gap differences between long and short cartridges. Long cartridges worked better. Full-metal-jacket rounds nearly stuck together, leaving matching copper circles at the contact point. Very low-powder loads either stalled in the barrel or still shattered. Firing at an aluminum Ridge wallet, the sponsor's product, produced a 22 g lump against a 14 g single bullet. Destin calls it a partial fusion that doesn't count. A true fusion is still unachieved, and he hints at further attempts. He suggests the fault is bullet speed and a lead alloy unlike Civil War lead.

For you, the practical points are mostly about method, since Destin repeatedly says not to copy the shot. First, to reproduce results in any risky or precise experiment, remove variable delays (here, swap mechanical lock time for electrical triggering), fix a rigid datum, and change one variable at a time. Second, write a procedure that covers failure modes in advance (misfires, hangfires, stray voltage), then have experienced people critique it before you run it.
