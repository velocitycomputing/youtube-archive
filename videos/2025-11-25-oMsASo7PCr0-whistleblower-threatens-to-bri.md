## Summary

Chief Judge James Boasberg resumed a contempt investigation into the administration over the alleged unlawful deportation of Abrego Garcia to El Salvador after a March 15 emergency order to stop or reverse removal flights. The transcript claims the administration used evasive legal arguments, including a false jurisdictional excuse, and that DOJ official Emil Bovay allegedly told staff they might have to defy the court and that “those planes are going to land no matter what.” Former DOJ lawyer Erez Reuveni was reportedly fired after admitting in court that the transfer violated a standing order, and he is now described as a whistleblower who submitted information to congressional leaders and the Inspector General. The DC Circuit paused the inquiry for seven months but later issued an opinion that, according to the speaker, allowed Boasberg’s contempt investigation to continue, while Bovay was rewarded with a Third Circuit judgeship despite confirmation testimony denying that he countermanded judicial orders.

Actionable follow-up is legal and civic rather than health-related: track Boasberg’s hearings, Reuveni’s testimony, the DC Circuit opinion, congressional or Inspector General records, Bovay’s confirmation record, and any Supreme Court action. Research leads include the legal standards for criminal contempt, the duty of candor to courts, whistleblower protections, and remedies against executive officials. Decisions to consider include whether to treat the whistleblower account as credible pending documentary proof, whether to monitor for a formal contempt finding, and whether to ask follow-up questions such as: what specific evidence supports the alleged order violation, whether Bovay’s statements are documented, whether Congress opens an investigation, whether his judgeship confirmation is challenged, and whether any enforceable punishment is possible.
