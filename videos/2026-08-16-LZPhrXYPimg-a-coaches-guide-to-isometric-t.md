## Summary

Matt, a doctor of physical therapy and strength coach with The Movement System, presents a spectrum of isometric training from long-duration general physical preparedness holds to short-duration maximal and neuromuscular performance work. He describes 1–3 minute holds such as split squat holds, wall sits, back extension holds, push-up holds, calf raise holds, glute bridges, and dead hangs, noting that the back extension hold relates to the Sorensen test, where holding longer than 60 seconds is associated with lower lower-back-pain risk, and that the Houston Texans begin off-season training with long holds, sometimes up to 3 minutes, before progressing to heavier loads and shorter durations. He then identifies 20–45 second holds, especially around 20–30 seconds, as a key range for tendon rebuilding, citing MRI examples of patellar tendon remodeling at 12 and 18 months and an Olympian’s remodeling in under 2 months. The key variables are time, tension, and volume: challenging holds around 60% of maximum force or RPE 7/10, with 3–6 sets of 30-second holds twice per week as a baseline, up to 6–8 sets as much as six times per week on the higher end, using exercises such as leg extensions, leg presses, Spanish squats for the patellar tendon, single-leg calf raises for the Achilles, long-lever bridges for hamstrings, band or wall external rotation for rotator cuff tendons, and starfish side planks or side plank leg lifts for glute med tendons. For performance, he contrasts yielding isometrics with overcoming isometrics, recommends 3–5 second maximal pushes into fixed objects such as a barbell, cites Bruce Lee’s historical 10-second maximal isometric pushes, and gives a protocol of 4 sets of 3 reps per side with 5–10 seconds between sides and 1–2 minutes between sets, noting an OVR ground-contact-time device showed about a 7% jump-height increase. He also covers neuromuscular maximum isometrics of 0.5–1 second for rate of force development, including ISO switches or ISO catches coined by Dr. Alex Natera, performed for about 10–12 seconds per set with 1–2 minutes rest and 3–4 quality sets per muscle group.

For a user, the actionable takeaway is to match the isometric type to the goal: use 1–3 minute holds for general tendon and tissue tolerance, especially for climbers, rowers, swimmers, or people returning from inactivity; use 20–45 second holds, especially 30-second holds at RPE 7/10, for tendon health, starting with 3–6 sets twice per week and increasing volume only if needed; and use 3–5 second overcoming isometrics before training for maximal force, followed by longer yielding holds afterward if tendon work is also needed. If the goal is explosive sprinting or sport-specific sharpness, only progress to 0.5–1 second neuromuscular isometrics or ISO switches after building through longer holds and max-force isometrics, because these require control and tolerance. Concrete decisions include choosing a tendon-specific exercise, adding load until a 30-second hold feels at least 7/10, tracking whether symptoms stay acceptable during and after holds, and using the Sorensen back-extension hold as a screening or conditioning target if lower-back-pain risk is a concern. Useful follow-up questions are whether pain is tendon-related or joint-related, whether the chosen exercise directly loads the target tendon, whether volume is too low or too high for the current stage, whether performance isometrics are being done before fatigue, and whether a clinician or coach should assess form before loading tendons heavily.
