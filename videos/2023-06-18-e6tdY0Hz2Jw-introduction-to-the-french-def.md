## Summary

Ben Finegold's kids' class introduces the French Defense (1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5), whose purpose is to get ...d5 in with the pawn already defended by ...e6, so Black doesn't have to move the queen. He covers White's main tries. The Exchange (exd5) gives a symmetrical, slightly better position for White. Nc3 and Nd2 both defend e4. The Advance (e5) creates a closed center where Black attacks White's d4 pawn with ...c5 and ...Qb6, and develops with ...Bd7. A trap to know: after ...Qb6, Bd3 appears to win a pawn but lets Black's queen get hit by Bb5+, so ...Bd7 is the right response. He also explains that moves like a3 (White) and ...a6 (Black, as in the Najdorf) are defensive prophylaxis against the opponent's pieces reaching b4/b5. Against 3.Nc3 he shows the Winawer pin (...Bb4), the Classical ...Nf6 and ...Be7 lines, and the McCutchen (...Bb4 after ...Nf6/Bg5). The key rule there: when a bishop pins your knight to your queen and a pawn attacks the knight, reply ...h6 and hit the pinning bishop. He mentions Alekhine's h4 pawn sacrifice and his own "Fort Knox" setup (...Bd7, ...Bc6). Along the way he tells stories about Fischer, Uhlmann-style grandmaster games, and Yuri Shulman. He closes with a general point: playing 1...e6 pulls opponents who only know 1.e4 e5 out of their comfort zone.

What's actionable: if you want a repertoire against 1.e4 that isn't 1...e5, learn the French as a structure, not a move list. Know that in the Advance, Black's plan is pressure on d4 via ...c5 and ...Qb6, and that ...Bd7 comes before grabbing anything after Bd3. Memorize the 3.Nc3 Bb4 4.e5 pin-and-pawn rule (play ...h6, attack the pinner), and don't take Alekhine's h4 pawn automatically, since over half of grandmasters decline it. Finegold's shortcut for learning an opening cheaply is to study the games of a top player who only plays it: for the French, look up Yuri Shulman's games, such as his win over Nakamura in the US Championship. Also look up the Fischer game from 1963 he mentions, search for the Winawer and McCutchen lines, and consider a quiet Fort Knox (...Bd7/...Bc6) if you want something solid with less theory. If you're White, try Bishop e3 against ...Qb6 in the Advance (the Bill Thompson approach) or ...a3 ideas to throw French players off their prep.
