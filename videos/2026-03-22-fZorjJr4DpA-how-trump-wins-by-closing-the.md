## Summary

Professor Helen Thompson argues on Unheard that the Iran war and Strait of Hormuz disruption should be read less as a failed U.S. operation and more as part of the Trump administration’s energy-geopolitical competition with China. She says oil markets outside Asia are underpricing risk, that normality may not return because the conflict has shattered assumptions for Gulf producers, and that the immediate trigger for “closure” was Lloyd’s of London shipping insurers halting Persian Gulf coverage after a U.S. strike on an Iranian naval ship near Sri Lanka, not only Iranian attacks. Thompson notes Hormuz transit mainly serves Asia, especially China; about 90% of Iranian exports went to China after Trump’s sanctions; Saudi Arabia may reroute up to a disputed 7 million barrels/day via a Red Sea pipeline, but Houthi attacks could negate that; China has built reserves and may turn to Russia via Power of Siberia, opened in 2014, or Arctic LNG, while burning more coal. She also says the U.S. is least exposed, Europe is more dependent on U.S. energy, JD Vance opposed U.S. Red Sea security, Russia is stronger because higher prices and eased sanctions let it sell to India and China at market prices, and the policy question for Keir Starmer and European leaders is whether to help secure Hormuz or accept U.S. disengagement from the Red Sea and Suez, recalling the 1967-1975 Suez closure.

For the user, the actionable takeaway is to treat Hormuz, the Red Sea, and the Suez Canal as linked chokepoints that can raise oil, LNG, fertilizer, and helium costs and disrupt supply chains, especially for Asia and Europe. Track shipping-insurance rates, tanker rerouting, Houthi activity, U.S./EU policy on Russian oil sanctions, China’s strategic oil reserves, China-Russia pipeline and Arctic LNG progress, and European LNG dependency; if making investment or procurement decisions, consider hedging energy exposure, reviewing fertilizer, helium, and medical-supply dependencies, and stress-testing budgets for a prolonged high-energy-price environment. Follow-up questions should ask whether the Trump administration deliberately tolerated Hormuz chaos to hurt China, whether Europe can build a coalition to secure the Red Sea without U.S. support, whether a Europe-Russia energy reset becomes politically possible, and whether energy-price shocks trigger recession or policy shifts in the next one to two quarters.
