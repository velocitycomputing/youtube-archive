## Summary

Eric Schmidt explained how modern warfare in Ukraine has become a continuous drone war where humans cannot survive exposed positions; Russia has innovated faster than expected by adding jet engines to Iranian-designed Shaheds, creating drones faster than current interceptors can engage. He outlined five escalating threats: increasing ballistic missiles (~1,300/year), jet-powered drones, scaled glide bombs, likely Russian mobilization of 500,000+ troops after September elections, and a Russian Starlink competitor launching next year that will enable autonomous drone coordination. Schmidt distinguished automation (computer vision targeting, already deployed) from true AI (reinforcement learning, not yet seen), noting that computers could eventually run autonomous drone swarms and develop unpredictable war strategies—his hope is mutual AI deterrence, though he doubts Russia and China will follow NATO rules.

Ukraine needs immediate Western technological and financial aid to counter jet-powered drones and ballistic missiles, particularly more Patriot interceptors—the innovation cycle operates on weeks, not years, so help must come quickly. The U.S. and NATO should actively capture Ukrainian battlefield innovations and drone-combat knowledge rather than leaving it isolated, since Russia will likely become a future adversary. Establish AI warfare governance now rather than waiting for crisis, similar to post-WWII nuclear control. Societally, prioritize protecting children from AI downsides as adoption accelerates, and prepare for cyberattacks as the likely first conflict vector.
