## Summary

Harari and the interviewer discuss AI as a new kind of agent rather than a mere tool, arguing that language has been civilization’s operating system since the cognitive revolution roughly 50,000–70,000 years ago and that AI may now surpass humans at manipulating language. They claim democracy is a large-scale conversation dependent on information technologies such as print, newspapers, radio, television, the internet, and social media, while dictatorship is a dictate; social media has already caused “earthquakes” in democracies by replacing human editors with algorithmic gatekeepers on platforms such as X, TikTok, and Instagram. Harari contrasts tools like Gutenberg’s printing press and the atomic bomb, which cannot decide or invent, with AI agents that can make decisions, invent new weapons, and potentially run non-human corporations; he cites Silicon Valley’s “create a god and keep it as a slave” contradiction, the hundreds of billions of dollars invested in AI, Argentina’s announced move to grant AI legal personhood, and examples such as Google, Facebook, Toyota, Mercedes-Benz, Lenin, Mussolini, China, the United States, Elon Musk’s Starlink in Ukraine, and the Roman and British empires to argue that AI could concentrate power through data, bureaucracy, and kill switches. He also discusses truth versus fiction, noting that truth is costly, complex, and often painful while fiction is cheap, simple, and flattering; he warns that AI may make finance and other systems so complex that the estimated 5% of humans who understand finance could fall to zero within a decade, reducing humans to the position of horses in a financial system they cannot perceive.

For the user, the actionable takeaway is to treat AI outputs as persuasive agents that can shape beliefs, emotions, and decisions, not neutral assistants: verify high-stakes claims with independent sources, require human review for legal, financial, medical, and career decisions, and avoid delegating judgment to AI in areas involving identity, relationships, health, or civic participation. Health-wise, the discussion points to risks of cognitive offloading, algorithmic manipulation, chronic uncertainty about truth, and stress from AI-mediated social environments; concrete responses include limiting AI-generated intimacy, auditing one’s information diet, preserving offline reflection, maintaining human editorial judgment, and watching for signs of learned helplessness or dependence on opaque systems. Research leads include AI legal personhood, non-human corporations, algorithmic media governance, deepfake detection, sovereign AI infrastructure, kill-switch dependencies, financial-system opacity, and the decline of human understanding of law and finance; decisions to consider include choosing AI vendors with auditability, disclosure of AI agents, data sovereignty, and human oversight, supporting journalism and scientific institutions, and asking follow-up questions such as: Who controls the model and data? Can decisions be audited? What happens if the system is disconnected? How can users detect AI impersonation? How can individuals and institutions preserve human comprehension of the systems that govern their lives?
