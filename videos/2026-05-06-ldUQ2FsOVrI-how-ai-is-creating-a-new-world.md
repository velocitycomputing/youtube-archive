## Summary

Ian Bremmer, founder of Eurasia Group and a Columbia political scientist, argues the world is in a “Gzero” or geopolitical recession: the U.S. is withdrawing from the global-policeman, free-trade, and institution-building roles it built after World War II, while no other power can or will replace it. He frames the moment as both cyclical—old postwar rules no longer match power—and technological, because AI is changing power faster than institutions can adapt. Named actors include the U.S., China, Canada, Europe, the U.N., the World Health Organization, Trump, Xi Jinping, Macron, Anthropic, Google, Fed Chair Powell, Treasury Secretary Bessent, and roughly 40 “essential” companies brought in after a major AI announcement. Key claims: the U.S. still has the reserve currency, global military projection, and leading AI/defense ecosystem; China is winning in some technology areas and making long-term moves on Taiwan, critical minerals, rare earths, and institutional influence, but is not yet a global superpower; the U.S.-China relationship is not a clean new Cold War because most countries hedge; and AI governance is dangerously thin, with U.S. AI companies acting with “functional sovereignty” while Chinese firms are captured by the state. Bremmer also cites Anthropic’s decision not to publicly release a model it reportedly saw as potentially existential for software-dependent infrastructure, warns that U.S. regulation is being shaped by lobbying and legal-liability dynamics, and says the AI race is the most important medium-term geopolitical question.

For practical use, treat this as a risk-and-decision map: monitor AI governance, U.S.-China tech decoupling, critical-mineral and rare-earth supply chains, sovereign-AI spending, reserve-currency stability, and whether AI power centralizes in a few platforms or decentralizes to agents and smaller developers. If you are personally affected by AI, audit which AI tools you rely on for work, money, health, or information, because Bremmer’s point is that users may form “the most important relationship” with AI while having little say over its rules; verify health, legal, and financial outputs with qualified professionals, protect private data, and assume AI systems may be shaped by business models rather than civic accountability. Research leads include “Gzero,” geopolitical recession, AI regulatory capture, legal liability as safety regulation, middle-power hedging, U.S.-China technology blocs, Anthropic’s safety decisions, and the World Health Organization’s role if the U.S. retreats from global institutions. Follow-up questions worth asking: Is AI becoming commodified or more concentrated? Which companies or governments can actually enforce safety standards? How will legal liability change AI deployment? Which allies are building credible third paths? And for health-related AI, who is accountable when a model affects diagnosis, treatment, insurance, or public-health messaging?
