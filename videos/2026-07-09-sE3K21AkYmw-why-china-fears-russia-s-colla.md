## Summary

Mehrdad Sharifjan of Eurasian Express argues that China’s caution about Russia’s war in Ukraine is shaped less by opportunism than by historical memory: Beijing fears Russian state collapse because past Russian revolutions, civil wars, famines, and imperial breakdowns repeatedly destabilized China’s western frontiers. The video cites the 1911 Qing collapse and Xinhai Revolution, loss of Outer Mongolia, Tibet’s weakened control, the 1916 Central Asian rebellion against Tsarist mobilization, Kazakh and Kyrgyz refugee flows into Xinjiang, warnings from Xinjiang governor Yang Zengxin to Nanking, Soviet operations in Xinjiang, the 1921 assassination of White Russian leader Ataman Alexander Dutov in Suidun, Chinese sheltering and later handing over White Russian commanders Nikolai Denisov and Boris Annenkov to the Soviets, Stalin’s 1930s collectivization and famine causing a possible one-third decline in Kazakh population, the 1944 Soviet incorporation of Tuva, the Soviet-backed East Turkestan Republic in Xinjiang, Stalin’s later shutdown of that project, Mao’s 1949 consolidation of Xinjiang, Gorbachev-era reforms influencing China’s 1989 atmosphere, and the 1991 Soviet collapse energizing Uyghur nationalism. The central claim is that China’s worst-case scenario is not a weakened Russia it can dominate, but a nuclear-armed, fragmented Russia with private armies, ethnic separatism, economic collapse, mass refugees, and uncontrolled weapons proliferation on its border.

Actionable takeaways are mainly research and risk-monitoring leads: track indicators of Russian state capacity, nuclear command stability, private military groups, border-control failures, Central Asian refugee movements, Xinjiang security policy, separatist rhetoric in Siberia/Tuva/Central Asia, and China’s diplomatic language about “stability,” “dialogue,” and “peace.” For decisions, anyone with travel, business, supply-chain, academic, or investment exposure to Russia, Central Asia, or China’s western regions should treat Russian instability as a geopolitical risk multiplier rather than a simple opportunity, and consider contingency plans for sanctions, border closures, migration shocks, or security crackdowns. Useful follow-up questions include: how strong are current private-army or regional-militia dynamics inside Russia; what evidence exists for ethnic separatism in Tuva, Siberia, or the North Caucasus; how is China preparing for possible refugee or weapons-proliferation spillover; and whether Beijing’s public calls for negotiations reflect genuine fear of Russian collapse or a strategy to preserve Russia as a buffer state while avoiding direct military responsibility.
