## Summary

Kyrylo Budanov, head of Ukraine’s military intelligence, argued that Ukrainian deep strikes on Russian refineries, gas-processing plants, substations, fuel depots and ammunition sites have inflicted more damage on Russia’s oil-supported war economy than Western sanctions, citing September seaborne oil-product exports falling 17.1% from August to 7.58 million tons, refined-product exports at their lowest since 2020, diesel exports at multi-year lows, and reports that up to 16 of Russia’s 38 refineries were struck since August. The transcript names attacks on a refinery in the Samara region, a gas-processing plant in Orenburg, a fuel and lubricants depot in occupied territory, an ammunition warehouse near the Kinburn Spit, a substation in the Ulyanovsk region, an ammunition plant in Bashkortostan, and targets in occupied Crimea, while also citing FT/Reuters reporting that U.S. intelligence helped Ukraine plan long-range drone routes, altitudes, timing and targeting. It frames Operation Spiderweb on June 1, in which Ukraine claimed 41 Russian aircraft were damaged or destroyed, as a template for the refinery campaign, notes Russia’s retaliation with one of its heaviest energy-grid attacks of 2025—reportedly 300+ drones and 37 missiles in one night causing blackouts in at least eight Ukrainian regions—and touches diplomacy with Budanov’s claim that no deal is possible without Ukraine, plus references to Keith Kellogg, Trump, Zelensky, Witkoff, and a promotional segment for the documentary series Kiev of Mine.

Actionable follow-ups include treating the video’s claims as research leads rather than settled facts: verify refinery outage and export figures through Reuters, the IEA, KSE Institute, Argus, Vortexa, Ukrainian General Staff or SBU briefings, and open-source energy trackers; monitor whether Ukraine expands strikes from refineries to gas processing, ports, pumping stations, rail fuel depots, thermal power plants and export chokepoints; assess implications for European energy security, fuel prices, sanctions enforcement, shadow-fleet shipping, Russian export bans and domestic price caps; and consider escalation risk if strikes broaden or if Russia continues mass attacks on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure. Follow-up questions should ask whether sanctions are being fully enforced, what strategic goals are tied to the strikes, how civilian harm is minimized, whether U.S. intelligence support is officially acknowledged, and whether the campaign is merely irritating Moscow or materially changing Kremlin calculations; if the user cares about health or humanitarian impacts, track blackout duration, heating and gas shortages, hospital disruptions, water and sanitation failures, and displacement rather than looking for personal medical advice.
