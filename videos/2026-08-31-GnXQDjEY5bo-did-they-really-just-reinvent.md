## Summary

The video is a hands-on review of a Shark guitar, which the reviewer had built at a factory in Antalya, Turkey, with founder Jim Cook (a former Turkish rockstar). The reviewer says it's unsponsored. The guitar's features are a two-pin swappable neck that takes 10–30 seconds to change (including a fretless neck), a titanium tremolo with ball-bearing rollers, a neck-angle adjustment, and full-range tuners. It also has solderless swappable pickups with coil-split volume compensation, onboard analog effects (compression, overdrive, fuzz) plus digital ones, and individually adjustable stainless steel frets. The tremolo held tuning very well, even after the neck was removed and refitted, and the frets let him fix a dead spot near fret 8. The downsides were these:
- The setup needed readjusting when he swapped necks.
- Very low action made the tone sound muffled and the strings feel dead during neck bends.
- The shallow string break angle over the rollers may cost some tone.
- The guitar was sensitive to temperature changes.
- A pickup connection crackled until the pins were cleaned (the company is moving them from brass to stainless steel).
- The effects can get dangerously loud.
- The rear-mounted effect controls were awkward to use.
- The digital effects weren't useful.

He admired the engineering and said it plays well. He still didn't enjoy it more than his existing guitars, and he noted that it has more variables to set up and uses only proprietary parts, so a local shop can't replace them. Prices run from roughly $2,000 to $5,000 depending on spec.

Whether this is actionable depends on whether you play guitar and are considering a Shark. If you are, the video suggests asking yourself whether you have the problems it solves: tuning drift from tremolo use, fret wear that needs refrets, or the need to travel with a guitar that comes apart. The effects-free version has the same neck, bridge and frets. It's a reasonable way to skip the weakest part, and the effects are probably the least compelling feature. Plan to learn the setup, because the adjustable frets, neck angle and truss rod can cause problems if you change them without knowing what you're doing. Check with the company before buying about how replacement parts are supplied. Also check whether the pickup-pin change to stainless steel is in the model you'd get. Try one in person if you can, particularly to check the low-action tone and the neck-bend feel. If you're happy with a well-set-up conventional guitar, the reviewer's own conclusion is that it solves problems on paper but doesn't clearly make playing better.
