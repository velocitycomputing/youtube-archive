## Summary

Dr. Mark D'Esposito, a neurologist and UC Berkeley professor, joined Andrew Huberman to discuss executive function, prefrontal cortex function, memory, working memory, dopamine, and cognitive control as the brain’s “CEO” system for planning, goal maintenance, rule application, attention, and flexible behavior. He described the frontal lobes as roughly one-third of the cortex, with about 25 subregions, and said they are the latest brain system to mature—into the 20s—and among the first to degrade with sleep deprivation, stress, normal aging, traumatic brain injury, concussion, stroke, Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, ADHD, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. Key claims included that the prefrontal cortex stores learned rules hierarchically, sends top-down signals to prioritize relevant information and suppress irrelevant input, and can fail in a way where patients know the correct rule but cannot apply it, as illustrated by French neurologist case examples of patients impulsively using objects in their environment. The discussion also covered the two-marshmallow task, delayed gratification, goal maintenance across time scales, and why many cognitive-training programs improve trained tasks but do not generalize unless tied to real-world projects; it named Brian Levine and colleagues at the Rotman Research Institute and their goal-management training as a promising approach. The transcript also included sponsor segments for Maui Nui Venison, Joovv, and Eight Sleep, with claims about roughly 1 gram of protein per pound of body weight, venison’s protein-to-calorie ratio, red/near-infrared light therapy, and sleep-related body-temperature changes of about 1–3°F.

Actionable takeaways: treat distractibility, poor prioritization, impulsivity, procrastination, and difficulty sustaining long-term goals as possible signs of impaired executive function, especially when sleep-deprived, stressed, aging, or recovering from brain injury, and prioritize sleep, stress reduction, and structured goal-setting before relying on supplements or drugs. To train cognitive function in a way more likely to transfer to real life, use concrete projects—such as planning a trip, meal, work task, or study plan—then explicitly define the goal, break it into subgoals, monitor progress, reduce distractions, manage anxiety, and review what worked; avoid assuming brain-training games alone will improve daily functioning. If symptoms are severe, persistent, or linked to ADHD, concussion/TBI, Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, mood disorders, or aging, seek clinical evaluation and ask about structured goal-management training, cognitive rehabilitation, neuropsychological testing, and whether medication or therapy is appropriate. Useful research leads include dopamine’s role in working memory, prefrontal top-down attention, environmental-dependency behaviors after frontal damage, and methods that make cognitive training generalize; follow-up questions should include how to adapt goal-management training for home use, how much sleep and stress control are needed for measurable cognitive improvement, and which interventions have evidence for specific diagnoses rather than generic “brain optimization.”
