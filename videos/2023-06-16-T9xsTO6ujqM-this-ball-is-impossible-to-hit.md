## Summary

Mark Rober visits his childhood home in Brea, California, and then plays against the Pro Wiffle Ball league in Oklahoma, where he loses to Jimmy "the Knorpedo" Knorp and founder Kyle. Their pitches move about 10 feet sideways. To work out why, he studies the pitchers at CrunchLabs, at the San Francisco Giants with pitching coach Brian Banister, and at Stanford. Stanford's batters face a normal baseball and an identical one with no seams. His explanation combines two ideas. The first is Newton's third law: a rocket, or an astronaut who throws a wrench, is pushed the opposite way. The second is the Coanda effect: air follows a smooth curved surface. A spinning ball drags air around the side where its surface moves with the airflow. That air gets deflected one way, so the ball is pushed the other way. This is the Magnus effect, and it applies to tennis balls, soccer balls, golf balls, baseballs and frisbees. Baseball seams grab more air and give a better grip, so the ball spins faster and curves more, and the seamless-ball test confirmed this. Wiffle balls curve toward their smooth side, away from the holes, and serious players scuff the holed side to make the effect stronger. For the rematch he builds gimmicks:
