## Summary

Sam Harris argues that the Democratic Party’s loss and Trump’s return were overdetermined but substantially tied to identity-politics messaging, citing his own post-election email apologizing as a “cisgendered white man” for existing as an example of the style he wants exorcised. He claims identity politics treats past unfairness as requiring new unfairness to reset equity, says most Americans are horrified by historical injustice yet want neighbors of every group to thrive, and uses the Daniel Penny–Jordan Neely subway case to argue that moral judgments become obscene when they change only after skin colors are known. He names Nicholas Kristof’s Yale Halloween-costume episode around 2014–2015 as a cultural turning point, invokes Martin Luther King Jr.’s character-over-color ideal, and says progressives now fixate on racial difference almost as much as white supremacists, making race mentions probably counterproductive and unethical unless proven otherwise.

For the user, the actionable move is to audit political and social messaging by asking whether it defines fairness in universal terms or in identity-group terms, and whether it improves measurable outcomes in health, education, wealth, and public trust. No direct health advice is given, but the health-adjacent research lead is to separate legacy structural harms from claims that conscious racism is actively holding groups back, then test whether identity-based framing increases stigma, polarization, or policy support. Decisions to consider include supporting candidates or organizations that emphasize broad fairness over race, gender identity, or religion as moral categories, and follow-up questions should ask: what metric proves a policy is fair, does mentioning race help or harm persuasion, how do we address disparities without treating superficial traits as morally decisive, and what evidence shows identity politics helps or hurts electoral and social outcomes?
