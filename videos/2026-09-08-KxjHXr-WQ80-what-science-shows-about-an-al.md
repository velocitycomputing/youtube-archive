## Summary

Brad Pitt’s Esquire interview about returning to drinking after seven years of sobriety anchors a discussion of whether someone with alcohol use disorder can drink safely again, separating “control” from “health.” The transcript cites Pitt’s 2012 comments about drinking “a Russian under the table,” his 2016 divorce from Angelina Jolie, his GQ account of drinking or smoking daily since college, his AA group’s emphasis on honesty, and Bradley Cooper’s continued sobriety after helping Pitt get sober. It reviews NESARC data on more than 4,000 formerly dependent adults: about 25% still dependent, 25% partly improved, 12% risky drinking without symptoms, 18% abstinent, and 18% low-risk drinking without dependence; Project MATCH follow-ups; the 2022 NIAAA recovery definition not requiring abstinence; and John Kelly’s 2025 study of about 500 people where roughly 35% met recovery criteria, two-thirds abstinent and one-third moderating, with abstainers functioning more stably and moderators having milder initial symptoms. It also covers DSM-5 alcohol use disorder severity, the Sobells’ controlled-drinking controversy and exoneration, Washingtonians/AA history, Bill Wilson’s belladonna/LSD/B3 interests, cancer risk via acetaldehyde, the 2025 Surgeon General advisory linking alcohol to seven cancers and about 100,000 annual U.S. cancer cases, conflicting 2025 federal reviews, abstainer bias, industry-funded trial scandal, aging physiology, medication interactions, falls, sleep disruption, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome mistaken for Alzheimer’s, Gallup 2025 data showing 24% drink, 44% abstainers, 2.8 weekly drinks, under-35s drinking less than adults 35+, and rising older-adult binge drinking and alcohol-induced deaths.

Actionable: if you or someone you know has ever had alcohol dependence, do not treat Pitt’s story as permission to test moderation; first assess severity using AUD criteria, withdrawal history, prior loss of control, medical or psychiatric comorbidity, medications, and social supports, ideally with an addiction clinician, because milder cases may sustain low-risk drinking while severe dependence carries high relapse risk. Consider evidence-based options: mutual-aid groups, therapy, medications for AUD if appropriate, structured monitoring, and a clear plan for lapses, remembering sober years still build skills and health. For older adults, review alcohol amount with a doctor because aging increases blood alcohol concentration, medication interactions, fall risk, and sleep disruption; ask about thiamine/B1 if heavy drinking plus confusion, memory loss, unsteadiness, or eye movement abnormalities occurs, and seek emergency care for sudden symptoms rather than assuming Alzheimer’s. Research leads to explore: NESARC, Project MATCH, Kelly 2025, NIAAA 2022 recovery definition, Surgeon General 2025 alcohol-cancer advisory, abstainer-bias corrections, and older-adult drinking trends; follow-up questions include “How many of the 11 AUD criteria were present?”, “Was there withdrawal?”, “What is the goal—abstinence or low-risk drinking?”, “What medications interact with alcohol?”, and “What non-alcoholic social connection can replace the 5 p.m. drink?”
