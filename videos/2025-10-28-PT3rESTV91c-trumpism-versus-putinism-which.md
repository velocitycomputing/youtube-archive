## Summary

Vlad Vexler and John discuss Trumpism and Putinism as expressions of a broader post-truth authoritarian wave rather than merely the pathologies of individual leaders. They argue that Western democracies are experiencing a historically “small to medium” transformation that feels enormous because it disrupts the unusually stable post-World War II and post-1989 order. Key claims include: politics is increasingly detached from evidence and shared reality; Russian propaganda weaponizes nihilism by flooding the information environment with contradictory claims to make citizens doubt truth itself; and Russian interference is real, especially since the 2014 annexation of Crimea, but should not be overestimated because domestic forces—social media, unregulated public squares, algorithmic incentives, oligarchic capitalism, economic exclusion, loss of historical memory, and hyper-individualism—are also eroding democratic trust. Named entities include Trump, Putin, Nigel Farage, the AfD, Russia, Ukraine, Crimea, and Oswald Mosley. Vexler argues that Putinism and Trumpism share monarchism, “might is right” hyperneoliberalism, and fascist connotations; both projects may collapse, but the ideological “wash” behind them will outlive them and likely produce more disciplined authoritarian successors. He also offers a key data point: if pro-democratic citizens remain sane and engaged, democratic collapse in the West by 2040 could be reduced from roughly 40% to 20%.

Actionable takeaways are both civic and health-related: treat information overload as a stress and mental-health issue by limiting doomscrolling, protecting sleep, building routines, and practicing “constructive grief” instead of paralysis or denial. Build epistemic skills by checking sources, comparing claims, noticing contradictions, distinguishing evidence from emotional mood, and studying Russian propaganda techniques such as denying the truth/lies distinction, inconsistency, saturation, and shallow relativism. In conversations with relatives or friends, prioritize relationship and intimacy first—spend most of the time on shared life concerns before trying to debate politics—and ask questions about concrete harms, values, and lived experience rather than trying to “win.” Decide how to participate locally: vote, attend community meetings, support pro-democratic institutions, and avoid blaming only Trump, Putin, or Farage while ignoring structural causes. Useful research leads include contagion versus convergence in post-truth politics, platform ownership and algorithmic incentives, electoral and institutional reform, UK/EU far-right movements, Russia’s domestic crisis, and the political aftermath of the Ukraine war. Follow-up questions to consider: Which domestic institutions are most brittle? Which platforms and algorithms are driving distrust? What reforms would make public discourse more accountable? How can citizens stay informed without becoming overwhelmed or politically numb?
