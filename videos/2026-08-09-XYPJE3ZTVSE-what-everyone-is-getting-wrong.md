## Summary

Russia is intensifying ballistic missile and converted air-defense strikes against Ukraine while Ukraine faces critical shortages of interceptor missiles like Patriot systems. However, the analysis shows this is far less catastrophic than media headlines suggest: even if Ukraine intercepted zero missiles for a year, Russia could damage only 1% of apartment buildings (Ukraine already lost 12–14% of housing stock to ground warfare), and Ukraine has proven resilient to power infrastructure attacks through distributed generators, solar systems, and invincibility stations that required only 13,000–50,000 uses across tens of millions of people in January 2026. Russia's strategy reveals critical vulnerabilities: they're importing North Korean technical teams to launch missiles (exposing expertise gaps), converting air-defense systems to offense (permanently degrading their own defenses), and Ukraine is successfully striking missile production facilities and launch teams.

Ukraine can mitigate this threat through multiple pathways: acquiring interceptors from allied nations, deploying the domestically-developed Freya missile system by 2027, striking Russian production facilities and launch crews, and continuing drone strikes on Russian infrastructure at rates exceeding Russia's own drone success. The key insight is that Russia appears to be gambling for a quick victory—a sign they recognize their advantage is temporary—while Ukraine's developing counter-capabilities and demonstrated resilience in previous winters indicate Ukraine is in the stronger long-term strategic position. Media narratives conflating human tragedy with strategic significance have obscured that Russia is spending down finite resources while creating permanent vulnerabilities.
