## Summary

Muscle building after 60 is framed as harder because hormones, muscle quality, joint stiffness, and anabolic resistance reduce responsiveness; the speaker says he helped thousands over 60 in six months through five changes: more rest, better exercise selection, full/stretched range of motion, separating fat loss from muscle gain, and training with appropriate intensity. He recommends 3–4 resistance sessions weekly for beginners and 4–5 for experienced lifters, with 48 hours between sessions and muscle groups, warns against the “stop-start” injury cycle, and contrasts isolation-heavy workouts with compound movements using a late-60s client’s routine: bodyweight squats to hack squat, chest flies to incline press, biceps curls to cable rows, triceps extensions to close-grip bench, deleting ab machine. He cites calf-raise research showing 3% gain with top squeeze, 6% with full range, and 15% with partial reps in the stretched position, though later research may temper that, and recommends lowering load by at least 25% to increase stretch. He also claims simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain is usually impossible except for beginners, very overweight, or genetically gifted people, recommends 100–200 calories above maintenance for muscle gain, and says intensity should be 1–3 reps in reserve, not failure, within a narrower recovery sweet spot for older adults; the “lifelong mobility program” is promoted.

Actionable: if over 60 and seeking muscle, first audit weekly training volume and recovery: cap at 3–4 sessions if new or aching, 4–5 only if experienced, and keep at least 48 hours between sessions and same muscle groups; if joint niggles appear, reduce volume before injury forces long layoff. Replace mostly isolation work with compound or multi-joint movements, but choose joint-friendly machines/cables and progress load gradually. Deliberately train through a controlled full range, especially the stretched position, by reducing weight about 25% and using exercises such as incline press, cable rows, close-grip bench, hack squat, Arnold press, or Romanian deadlifts as tolerated. Decide whether the priority is muscle gain or fat loss; if muscle gain, track daily weight and aim for a small 100–200 calorie surplus rather than cutting, and if fat loss is the priority, expect slower muscle gain. Use 1–3 reps in reserve on most sets, stopping when the last rep is tough but not collapse-level, and adjust based on soreness, sleep, joint pain, and performance. Follow-up questions/research leads: verify stretch-mediated hypertrophy evidence for older adults, protein intake and medical constraints, medication-related muscle/joint issues, and whether a clinician or physical therapist should screen for injury risk before increasing intensity.
