## Summary

The video lists ten "hidden" improvisation mistakes the instructor says he sees intermediate guitarists make. (1) They leave the scale before they know its shape well enough to play it confidently. (2) They leave the "box" (the minor pentatonic or minor scale shape) before they can outline chord tones inside it, for example over Em–G–D–C. (3) They start every line on beat one. (4) They always start lines on the scale's root, which he links to practicing scales only from the root. (5) Their single-note lines are rhythmically sloppy, which he calls the worst of the ten. He says the cause is uncertainty, and that you need to know what you'll play a few beats before you play it. (6) They play faster than they can manage. He says to know your "16th-note max", the fastest tempo where you play 16ths cleanly. The same speed then converts to other subdivisions: a 100 BPM max means 8ths at 200, triplets at about 133, and sextuplets at about 67. (7) They abandon a motif instead of developing it, which comes down to weak fretboard knowledge. (8) They leave no silence. (9) They restart at every chord change when the progression modulates between keys, leaving "no man's land" at the change. (10) They never practice with constraints, such as staying in one position, playing only 8th notes, or leaving more space. The video ends with a pitch for his paid "52 Week Guitar Player" program, with waitlist access on June 10 and public enrollment on June 13.

For you, the most useful steps are to record yourself improvising and check it against this list, then pick one or two mistakes and design a constraint drill for each. Examples:
- Start every phrase on a beat other than one, or on a scale degree other than the root.
- Practice the scale starting from each of its notes.
- Stay in one box and target the chord tones of each chord in a progression.
- Limit yourself to 8th notes, or leave a bar of silence between phrases.
- Repeat and vary a single motif for a whole chorus.

Find your 16th-note max with a metronome and keep your fast passages within it. Before moving on to new scale shapes, make sure you can play the one you have cleanly, and keep practicing chord-tone targeting inside it. For tunes that change key, practice the chord changes slowly so you can play through the transitions without stopping.
