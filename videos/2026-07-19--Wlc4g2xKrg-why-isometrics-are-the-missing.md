## Summary

Keith Baar explains that isometrics—muscle contractions without changing joint angle—are highly efficient and can be performed daily with minimal fatigue. The discussion covers how elite sprinters maintain top speed through isometric muscle contractions that store and return energy via tendons, while distance runners perform better when they have high passive stiffness (flexibility in resting positions) combined with active stiffness (muscle-generated tension). Research shows that stretching alone has zero effect on injury rates, whereas heavy weight lifting reduces muscular-skeletal injuries by two-thirds. The key insight is that injury risk follows a U-shaped curve: both excessive stiffness and excessive flexibility increase injury rates, and the optimal approach involves tension and length together rather than passive relaxation.

Apply this by adding isometric holds to your daily routine—hold positions like lunges, squats, or loaded stretches for time under tension rather than passively relaxing into stretches. If mobility work is your goal, use PNF-style stretching (contracting antagonist muscles while in lengthened positions) or simply hold challenging positions, which signal proper adaptation to your nervous system. Prioritize heavy resistance training over flexibility work for injury prevention. For runners and athletes seeking efficiency, develop passive stiffness by practicing resting positions (crouch, deep squat) while maintaining muscular tension, and avoid the gymnastics-style passive stretching that predisposes athletes to tendon ruptures.
