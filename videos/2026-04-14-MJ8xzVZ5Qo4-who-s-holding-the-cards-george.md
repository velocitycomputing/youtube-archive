## Summary

George Friedman and Christian Smith analyze the Iran war stalemate after Donald Trump’s “a whole civilization will die tonight” post, a tenuous ceasefire, negotiations in Pakistan, a US blockade of Iranian ports, Iran’s retained enriched uranium, the mostly closed Strait of Hormuz, and the continuing Lebanon war. Friedman argues the war will end through negotiations because neither the US nor Iran can win militarily, comparing bargaining to house sales, corporate deals, and the Vietnam/Paris peace talks involving Lyndon Johnson, Nixon, and Kennedy. He claims the US has more cards—nuclear power, military reach, and greater tolerance for $100 oil—but is constrained by public opinion, alliance politics, and Trump’s anti-long-war pledge; Iran is weakened but protected by the Revolutionary Guard/IRGC, a large territory (about 2.5 times Texas), and the political risk that capitulation could destabilize the regime. He identifies China as the decisive external actor because it needs Hormuz oil and a May US-China summit for export access, while Russia lacks logistics; Iran’s nuclear refusal signals a desire for deterrence and regional dominance over Saudi Arabia and Gulf states; and NATO is strained because Trump says allies did not join and the UK declined Hormuz blockade support.

Research leads include the Vietnam Paris peace talks as a negotiation model, the Iran-China economic relationship, US-China trade talks, IRGC control mechanisms, and verification options for Iranian nuclear material. Decisions to consider include hedging exposure to oil, shipping, defense, and China-linked supply chains if the user has financial or operational stakes; asking follow-up questions such as whether Iran will surrender nuclear material, whether China will pressure Iran instead of the US, whether the ceasefire survives, whether the IRGC remains cohesive, and whether the war in Lebanon expands.
